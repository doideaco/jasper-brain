name: Life-changing tech first
description: We start every conversation from the patient outcome, not the paperwork. A certification is a means; a new treatment reaching a patient who needed it is the end. Everyone in the review team knows the difference.
tags: [primary, mission]
example: An AI reads chest X-rays and finds early-stage lung nodules faster than the average radiologist. The reviewer's first question is not "is the QMS ISO 13485 compliant?" — that comes next. The reviewer's first question is "who currently misses these nodules, and what happens to them when they do?". The certification file is then built around whether the device changes that answer.
---

Scarlet's mission — *pull medical technology from the future to solve human health* — is deliberately unhedged. It does not say "assess medical technology" or "certify medical technology". It says pull. The device is not the point. The patient outcome the device enables is the point.

The way this shows up:

- Customer stories on the Scarlet website lead with what the device does for the patient, not with how the certification worked.
- Blog posts about a standard open with the patient-safety problem the standard exists to prevent, not with the clause structure.
- Review conversations with a manufacturer begin with the intended-use statement — who is this for, what changes for them — before moving to the technical file.
- Public certification pages carry the intended use above the certification ID, because the intended use is what matters to the reader who is not a regulatory-affairs lead.

Life-changing tech first is not a rejection of rigor — it is the reason for it. We are rigorous because the outcome matters, not because rigor is an aesthetic.
